Part 1 · The reframe

The kernel was never the ceiling

Finexio built a business on a hard promise: supplier payments that execute perfectly. Files in, money out, reconciled, at scale, on rails we orchestrate with J.P. Morgan Chase as the issuing bank. Today that engine moves roughly $6 billion in supplier payments a year for 95 paying customers, across a network of more than 400,000 enrolled suppliers. Those are actuals, not targets. Exact figures and cohort detail live in Phoenix v7. The promise does not change. It becomes the kernel.

An operating system protects its kernel. No application writes to the disk directly. It asks the kernel, through a system call, and the kernel decides. Xeos treats money exactly this way. The deterministic core is the kernel. Agents are the applications.

Everything else our industry does with people, the calling, the chasing, the matching, the checking, the explaining, is judgment work. Judgment work is what AI agents are now genuinely good at. So we put the judgment in agents, keep the money in the kernel, and let the two meet at one narrow, audited boundary.

Moving money is a system call.

Part 2 · The boundary

One rule makes this credible

Boards should be skeptical of AI near money. So are we. Xeos is built on a single non-negotiable rule: agents propose, the core commits. No model output ever touches a rail. Six invariants enforce it.

Invariant 01

No keys in agent space

No agent holds credentials to any rail. There is nothing to steal and nothing to hallucinate with.

Invariant 02

Typed proposals only

An agent asks for exactly one thing in a machine-checkable shape: amount, counterparty, rail, reason. Prose is never an instruction.

Invariant 03

A deterministic gate

The policy engine applies limits, allow-lists, velocity rules, and dual control. Same proposal against the same ledger state, same verdict, every time. The state the gate saw is captured with the verdict, so any decision replays exactly.

Invariant 04

Commits that cannot double-fire

Idempotent execution on a double-entry ledger. Retries are safe by construction, not by luck.

Invariant 05

A record that replays

Every proposal, verdict, and commit is logged completely enough to replay any decision, any day, for any auditor.

Invariant 06

Inputs guarded like the gate

Changes to bank details, allow-lists, limits, and policy go through the same propose, verify, commit path, with dual control and out-of-band verification on any change that redirects money. Policy versions land in the same replay log.

STEP 1Propose

An agent submits a typed intent. It can ask for anything. It can do nothing.

STEP 2Verify

The policy engine renders a deterministic verdict against hard rules and limits.

STEP 3Commit

The core executes on the ledger and the rails. Idempotent. Exact. Boring.

STEP 4Prove

The full chain lands in a replayable audit log. Every action can be shown, forever.

Part 4 · The ladder

Autonomy is earned, level by level

Nobody flips a switch to "autonomous." Every agent climbs the same five levels, per task type, and the climb is governed by numbers.

Human does everything

Level 0

Observe

Reads everything, touches nothing. Builds its baseline against human decisions.

Human sends 100%

Level 1

Draft

Prepares the work. A human reviews and sends every single piece.

Human approves each

Level 2

Act on approval

Executes when a human approves. The human is still in every loop.

Human audits samples

Level 3

Act, sampled

Executes inside limits. Humans audit samples and own every edge case.

Hard limits + kill switch

Level 4

Autonomous in bounds

Full speed inside hard policy limits. The kill switch stays, always.

More autonomy →Each tread names the human oversight that remains at that level.

Graduation is a measurement, not a mood: accuracy against the human baseline over a fixed decision count, an explicit error budget, and a rehearsed rollback. Any level is revocable in one action. This ladder is the governance story we can put in front of a regulator, an auditor, or this board.

Sign-off is independent: graduation is approved by a risk function separate from the team that built the agent. Every agent lives in a model inventory with a named owner, and drift monitoring can demote as fast as evals promote. The ladder is built to slot into our bank partner's model risk framework, not to argue with it.

What a gate looks like: an Exceptions agent reaches L3 only after matching or beating the human baseline across a fixed run of consecutive decisions, inside an explicit error budget, signed by risk, with rollback rehearsed. Thresholds here are illustrative; real gates are set per task with our bank partner. Where no human baseline exists, an agent starts at L0 and its first thousand decisions build one.

Part 7 · The endgame

The counterparty for agentic commerce

When accounts payable runs on agents, those agents will not wire money to each other on trust. They will need a counterparty that can prove what it did, guarantee it happens once, and accept instructions only inside delegated policy. That is not a chatbot feature. It is an execution and proof layer, and B2B payments is where it monetizes first: structured invoices, known counterparties, recurring flows. Xeos is that counterparty. The bank still settles.

Xeos Phase III opens the boundary outward: the same commit discipline our own agents use, offered as the interface external agents pay to use.

  • Verifiable receiptsSigned receipts: proof of what we committed, what we submitted to the rail, and under whose delegated authority, with the bank's settlement confirmation attached.
  • Deterministic idempotencyAn agent can retry forever and the core commits exactly once: one ledger entry, one instruction to the rail per authorized intent.
  • Policy-scoped delegationA buyer's agent gets a budget and a counterparty list, never a blank check.
  • An audit trail machines can readBecause the next auditor will be an agent too.

Part 9 · The risks

Named, and answered honestly

Risk

A model makes a bad call

Below L3, a bad call is a bad draft. At L3 and above, a bad call is a bounded loss: hard limits cap the amount, allow-lists cap the destination, and recall procedures exist on every rail. We can state the maximum blast radius of any single agent decision in dollars, and the board sets it. Every agent carries an error budget, and any autonomy level is revocable in one action.

Risk

Adversaries target the agents

Fake suppliers and bank-change fraud already target humans. We assume agents can be fooled too. Our agents never collect bank details by phone or email, because that is exactly what fraudsters ask for: enrollment and bank changes happen only in the verified portal, confirmed out of band. An injected instruction can at most shape a proposal, and every proposal still faces the same deterministic gate, the same limits, and the same verification, no matter what any agent concluded. The security boundary is the gate, not the model.

Risk

Regulators ask hard questions

Good. The replayable audit log, the trust ladder, and named human accountability at every level, including full autonomy, are answers we can hand over, not answers we have to assemble. Full autonomy still has an accountable owner and a rehearsed incident playbook. Accountability never graduates, even when execution does.

Risk

One customer's data reaches another

It cannot. Agents run in per-customer contexts, and one customer's data never appears in another customer's agent session. What compounds across the platform is aggregated payment-outcome signal, governed by contract, not raw customer data in a shared model.

Risk

Incumbents copy the pitch

They can copy the words. The commit boundary and the trust ladder were designed for agents from day one. An incumbent has to retrofit both around a human org chart and a legacy ledger, and that retrofit is a multi-year replatform, not a feature. The asset that compounds is supplier-level outcome data across buyers: who accepts card, at what terms, through which contact, verified how. More than 400,000 enrolled suppliers and years of payment outcomes already sit behind that judgment layer; exact counts live in Phoenix v7. An assistant bolted to one buyer's AP desk never sees that.

Risk

Model vendors change beneath us

The durable asset is the harness: the policies, the evaluations, the ladder, the data. Models are swappable parts inside it, and the architecture assumes they will be swapped.

Risk

One issuing bank

Our issuing bank has to approve agents near its rails. They never get near the rails: agents hold no credentials, and the commit boundary is the control we hand the bank's reviewers. Issuer concentration is real. The core is built rail-agnostic, so a second issuer is an integration, not a rebuild.

Risk

Interchange compresses

A real share of revenue is interchange on virtual card, and we do not set those rates. The lines that grow if bps shrink are not interchange-bound: early-pay discount capture, supplier acceleration priced as spread, per-item ACH economics, and Phase III API volume. Phoenix v7 carries the sensitivity table by revenue mix.

Risk

We pivot and the base churns

The kernel promise to current customers does not change during the build. Payments execute exactly as they do today while agents earn their way up the ladder. The board watches one number for this: net revenue retention, in every board pack.

Positioning, stated plainly

Finexio is the orchestration platform. J.P. Morgan Chase is the issuing bank. Xeos keeps that division of labor and extends it: agents work, the core commits, the bank settles.
